Vienna, 1770. In the palace of Schönbrunn, in front of Empress Maria Theresa, a court official called Wolfgang von Kempelen opens the doors of a wooden cabinet. Inside you can see wheels, gears, levers. Sitting behind the cabinet is a life-size figure dressed as a Turk, with a turban and a long pipe in his hand, looking at a chessboard. Kempelen closes the doors, winds up the mechanism with a key and the figure starts to move the pieces.

And wins.

It will go on winning for decades, under different owners, across half of Europe and America. According to the chronicles, Napoleon and Benjamin Franklin sat down to play it. And all that time there was a man inside: a good chess player, tucked into a sliding seat that hid him whenever the doors were opened, who followed the moves thanks to some magnets under the board and moved the figure’s arm with a system of levers.

The astonishing thing about the Turk is not the trick. It is how many people preferred to believe it.

More than two hundred and fifty years later you type a question to ChatGPT, it answers in impeccable Spanish, and the doubt is the same as in Schönbrunn: is there anybody in there? This article is the long answer. The short one, so you are not left in suspense: there is no gentleman hidden away. There are a great many more.

It is written for two people. For the one who nods when they say “ask the AI”, like someone who has it all under control, even though inside they have no idea what is behind it. And for the one who, in every family, used to set the video recorder, fixes the printer and receives from their aunt a four-minute WhatsApp voice note begging for help with the laptop. The first will leave here understanding. The second, able to explain it.

## Almost three thousand years trying to make somebody

Since the end of 2022 it seems that artificial intelligence is ChatGPT. It is and it isn’t. ChatGPT is the tool that made it popular overnight, and the one that turned the know-it-all uncles of the world into experts in something they did not understand but thought they did. The idea of making a mind, on the other hand, is as old as myths. Let me tell you how we got here. The timeline is mine, and like every timeline it is arbitrary: others would pick other moments.

Four attempts cross in that line: imitating the body, imitating reasoning, imitating the brain and, finally, letting the machine learn by itself. The automata left machines that seemed alive and, above all, people ready to believe that they were. Logic left the conviction that thinking is following rules and that it would be enough to write them all down: it worked for chess, not for recognising a cat. And neuroscience left the two pieces that are inside ChatGPT: the neuron as a building block and learning as the adjustment of connections.

What we call AI today is, almost always, the fourth attempt: machines that learn from examples. So let us open the cabinet.

## What is inside the cabinet

<strong>Artificial intelligence</strong> is an enormous name for something that today, in practice, almost always means the same thing: programs that, instead of following instructions written by a person, have <strong>learned from examples</strong>. That is machine learning.

Try it. Try to explain to someone, step by step and without mistakes, how to recognise a cat in a photo. Pointed ears? There are cats with floppy ears. Whiskers? Dogs have them too. Nobody knows how to write those instructions. So it is done the other way round: the machine is shown millions of photos, some with a cat and some without, and it is left to adjust its own insides until it gets it right.

Those insides are a <strong>neural network</strong>: a huge number of switches connected to one another that imitate, from very far away, Cajal’s neurons. What is adjusted is the strength of each connection, and those adjustments are called <em>weights</em> or <em>parameters</em>. The biggest models have trillions: millions of millions. They are the cabinet’s gears, only nobody has placed them by hand.

### The model, a recipe nobody can read

When the training ends, what is left is called a <strong>model</strong>. Think of a recipe the machine has written by itself after trying millions of dishes: it works, but nobody, not even those who trained it, can read it line by line. A company trains the model once, over months and spending a fortune on electricity, and then it is used millions of times a day.

If the model has been trained on text (books, newspapers, websites, forums, code) and is very large, it is called an <strong>LLM</strong>, from the English <em>large language model</em>. ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are LLMs with a chat window in front.

### A machine for guessing what comes next

What an LLM has learned to do is a single thing: given a text, <strong>guess what comes next</strong>. Like the keyboard on your phone, which when you type “good” suggests “morning”, but having read an entire library and looking at the whole conversation, not just the last word. It guesses a piece, adds it and guesses again. That way, piece by piece, a paragraph comes out.

Notice that I say “piece”. The model does not read words: it reads <a href="https://platform.openai.com/tokenizer"><strong>tokens</strong></a>, pieces of text that are sometimes a whole word, sometimes half of one and sometimes a punctuation mark. “Hola” is usually one token; “inteligencia” can be two or three. In English, a token comes to about three quarters of a word; in Spanish, somewhat less, because these systems were trained mostly on English texts and chop up other languages less well.

It looks like an engineer’s curiosity, but it explains three things you are going to notice. That companies <strong>charge by tokens</strong>, not by sentences. That each model can hold only a limited number of tokens in its head at once, and that is why in very long conversations it forgets the beginning. And that a machine that writes so well struggles to count the letters in a word or to write it backwards.

It does not see letters. It sees pieces.

### So, who is inside?

Now I can answer the question from Schönbrunn. Inside the model nobody is sitting. But what it has learned, it has learned from everything we people have written: millions of books, articles, forums, recipes and arguments. And before opening it to the public, OpenAI turned to people who <a href="https://openai.com/index/chatgpt/">wrote sample conversations and scored which answers were better</a>, to teach it manners. When ChatGPT answers you, it is not a hidden gentleman speaking to you. It is an average of a great many people.

That explains the good and, as we are going to see, also the bad.

Between the first ChatGPT and the first model that reasons, less than two years go by. And when one company launches an idea, the others have it within a few months. That is why any “the best one is X” goes out of date before you finish the sentence.

### Who makes this and why there are so few of them

Training one of the biggest models costs tens or hundreds of millions in computers and electricity, so the ones that matter come from a handful of companies. It is worth telling the company apart from its product:

- OpenAI makes the GPT models; <strong>ChatGPT</strong> is the application for talking to them. Microsoft, which has invested a lot of money in the company, uses them in its <strong>Copilot</strong>.
- Anthropic makes <strong>Claude</strong>.
- Google DeepMind makes <strong>Gemini</strong>, which is already inside the search engine and Android.
- Meta, the one behind Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp, made <strong>Llama</strong> and now makes <strong>Muse</strong>.
- Outside the United States, the French Mistral and the Chinese DeepSeek have shown that you can compete with less money.

If someone at dinner says “Facebook’s AI”, they mean Meta AI; if they say “Google’s ChatGPT”, Gemini. Do not correct them out loud. Smile.

Most of these models are closed: the cabinet is not opened, it is only used through its owner’s service. But some have <strong>open weights</strong>: their parameters — the recipe — are published so that anyone can download them and run them on their own computer, without asking permission and without their data leaving home. Llama, DeepSeek, Qwen, Kimi and a good part of Mistral’s models are open; the GPTs of ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are not, although OpenAI and Google also publish some smaller open models (gpt-oss and Gemma). They are shared on <a href="https://huggingface.co/">Hugging Face</a>, which is more or less the public library of models. Two caveats: “open” almost never includes the texts it was trained on, and to run one of the big ones you need a computer that is not your nephew’s.

### Eyes, ears and time to think

Until recently, all of this was text. Not any more. Today’s models are <strong>multimodal</strong>: they understand images, audio and, more and more, video. You take a photo of the electricity bill and ask what that charge is; you show it the plant on your balcony so it can tell you what is wrong with it. Inside it is the same thing, because the image is also cut into pieces and treated as tokens. For the person using it, everything changes.

The other change is <strong>reasoning models</strong>. A normal LLM answers off the cuff, like someone saying the first thing that comes to mind. A reasoning one, before replying, <strong>writes itself a draft</strong>: it breaks the problem down, tries one path, sees it does not add up, tries another. It takes longer and spends more tokens, but in maths, logic, programming or anything with several steps it makes far fewer mistakes. Careful with the word “thinks”: it is still guessing tokens, only first those of the draft and then those of the answer. A simplification, but an honest one: the trick works.

## Why it gets things wrong with such assurance

Now you can understand the whole sentence: <strong>an LLM produces the most probable text, not the truest</strong>. Nobody has taught it what truth is; it has been taught how a text usually continues. Almost always, the probable and the true coincide. Almost.

When they do not coincide, the model does not keep quiet or say “I don’t know”: it writes something that sounds perfect and is false. It is called a <strong>hallucination</strong>: a book that does not exist, with its author and its year; an invented court ruling; a quotation nobody said. And it says it with the same certainty as what is true, because for it there is no difference. Both were probable.

It happens more where there was less text to learn from: with exact figures and dates, with proper names, with the very recent (the model learned up to a certain date and does not know what came after, unless the application searches the internet for it) and with the very local. Ask it, without letting it search the internet, about the Falla final of a particular year, and check what it tells you.

And then there are <strong>biases</strong>, which are not a fault of the machine but a mirror. Remember who is inside? All of us. The model learns what we have written over centuries and also what we took for granted without realising. If in the texts nurses are almost always “she” and engineers “he”, the model will tend to repeat it. It has no prejudices of its own: it has ours, and sometimes magnified.

None of this makes it useless. It makes it what it is: an assistant that has read more than anyone, that writes better than almost everyone, that never gets tired and that, every now and then, comes out with an outrageous howler with total assurance. Treat it that way. What matters to you, check against another source. And ask it about something you know very well, your neighbourhood or your trade, and count the mistakes: it is the best way to know how far to trust it on what you do not know.

## What you tell it goes into the cabinet too

When you write something in a <em>chatbot</em>, it does not stay on your phone: it <strong>travels to a company’s computers</strong>, almost always in another country. What it does with it depends on each service and its terms, which change every few months. In the best-known free and paid services, unless you turn it off, your conversations may end up among the texts the next model is trained on. That is: inside the cabinet.

Open-weights models, running on your own computer, are the exception, but it is not what almost anybody uses. So a simple rule applies: <strong>if you would not say it out loud on the bus, do not type it</strong>. The <a href="https://www.aepd.es/prensa-y-comunicacion/notas-de-prensa/aepd-publica-decalogo-recomendaciones-proteger-privacidad-al-usar-ia">Spanish Data Protection Agency</a> recommends the same in its decalogue “Cuidado con lo que le confIAs”. Specifically, better not to tell it:

- Passwords or banking codes.
- Medical data, yours or other people’s.
- Identity documents, yours or your children’s.
- Secrets from your work or your clients’ data.
- Photos of other people without their permission, now that models can see.

Almost every service has in its settings an option so that your conversations are not used for training, another to delete the history and, sometimes, a “temporary chat” that is not kept in your history or used for training (although the company keeps it for a few days for security). Go and have a look at them. If you use an AI at work, ask first what your company allows: what for you is a silly question may be, for it, a leak of information. And you are not alone: since 2024 the European Union has had an <a href="https://www.europarl.europa.eu/topics/es/article/20230601STO93804/ley-de-ia-de-la-ue-primera-normativa-sobre-inteligencia-artificial">artificial intelligence regulation</a> that, applied in phases, outright bans some uses and obliges them to tell you when you are talking to a machine.

<strong>Try it:</strong> open the settings of the application you use and look for three things: where the history is deleted, what is done with your conversations and whether there is a temporary mode that does not leave it in the history.

## What it can do for you and what it cannot

With everything above, you can almost work this out yourself.

It is very good at <strong>working with words</strong>, because that is what it is made of: summarising a long text, explaining something difficult in another way, making a first draft, translating, suggesting ideas or putting yours in order. And, since it can see, <strong>looking at something and telling you about it</strong>: the bill, the sign in another language, the error message on the screen.

It is bad at, or should not do on its own, everything that demands <strong>being sure</strong>: deciding for you, giving you a definitive medical or legal diagnosis, replacing someone who really knows. It produces the probable, not the true. Use it to prepare the questions, not as the final answer; the <a href="https://uned.libguides.com/c.php?g=723100&amp;p=5318109">UNED’s guide to the critical use of AI</a> explains it well if you want an academic source. And if the task has several steps, a calculation or a comparison, switch on the mode that reasons.

That leaves the fear for jobs. It would be dishonest to promise you that nothing will change: things will change, as the computer and the internet changed them. But nobody knows exactly how or when, and whoever claims to with great assurance is doing the same thing as a language model: stating the probable as if it were true.

<strong>Try it:</strong> pick a heavy task from this week (a difficult email, a summary, organising a trip) and see whether it lends you a hand. If not, no harm done.

## You are the player

Let us go back to Schönbrunn, in case someone asks you while you serve dessert.

AI is not new. We have been trying for almost three thousand years to make something that looks alive, from Hephaestus’s golden maidservants to Vaucanson’s duck, and for centuries we have been willing to believe we have managed it. What we have now are not puppets or tin brains: they are <strong>programs that learn from examples</strong>. Language models, like ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini, guess the next piece of a text; they read <strong>tokens</strong>, not letters; some are closed and some <strong>open</strong>; some already <strong>see</strong> and others <strong>stop to think</strong>. And they get things wrong with an enviable assurance, because the probable is not always the true. What you tell them, besides, travels to somebody’s computers.

When in 1958 the newspaper promised a machine that would walk, talk and be conscious of its existence, it was wrong in the same way as your know-it-all uncle when he says AI is going to take over the world, or that it is nonsense that is no use for anything: mistaking the puppet for what it has inside.

In the Turk, whoever decided each move was hidden, in the dark, inside the cabinet. With AI it is the other way round. Inside goes the echo of millions of people who wrote before you. And whoever decides the move is outside, in front of the board, in plain sight of everybody.

It is you.
