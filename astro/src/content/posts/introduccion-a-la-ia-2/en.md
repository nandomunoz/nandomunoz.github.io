Vienna, 1770. In Schönbrunn Palace, in front of Empress Maria Theresa, a court official called Wolfgang von Kempelen opens the doors of a wooden cabinet. Inside you can see wheels, gears, levers. Sitting behind the cabinet is a life-size figure dressed as a Turk, with a turban and a long pipe in his hand, looking at a chessboard. Kempelen closes the doors, winds up the mechanism with a key and the figure starts moving the pieces.

And wins.

It will keep winning for decades, under different owners, across half of Europe and in America. According to the chronicles, Napoleon and Benjamin Franklin sat down opposite it. And all that time there was a man inside: a good chess player, tucked into a sliding seat that hid him whenever the doors were opened, who followed the moves thanks to magnets under the board and moved the figure's arm with a system of levers.

What is astonishing about the Turk is not the trick. It is how many people preferred to believe it.

More than two hundred and fifty years later you type a question to ChatGPT, it answers in impeccable Spanish, and the doubt is the same as in Schönbrunn: is anyone in there? This article is the long answer. The short one, so you are not left in suspense: there is no little man hidden away. There are a great many more.

## Almost three thousand years of trying to make someone

Since the end of 2022, for a lot of people artificial intelligence is ChatGPT. That is understandable: it was the tool that took it out of the laboratories and put it on any phone. But the idea of building a mind is much older than that date, and than computers. This timeline gathers some of the milestones that have led here:

Four paths cross in that list: imitating the body, imitating reasoning, imitating the brain and, lastly, letting the machine learn on its own. Almost everything we now call artificial intelligence takes the fourth, and it inherited its two pieces from neuroscience: the neuron as the building block and learning as the adjusting of connections.

## What is inside?

Programs that learn from examples, instead of following instructions written one by one by a person. Notice what I am not saying: nothing about thinking or understanding. They learn in a <strong>neural network</strong>, a mesh of artificial neurons in which each neuron is a small switch that receives numbers and passes others on to the next ones. Each connection has a value, the <strong>weight</strong>, which measures how much it influences. Learning is moving those weights a little at a time until it gets things right, with the rules that already appeared in the timeline: <a href="https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teor%C3%ADa_hebbiana">Hebb's</a>, <a href="https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perceptr%C3%B3n">the perceptron's error correction</a> and <a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/323533a0">backpropagation</a>. The biggest models have trillions of weights, and nobody has set them by hand.

The lesson can be a photo labelled “cat” or a reward for winning a game, as in <a href="https://doi.org/10.1147/rd.33.0210">Samuel's draughts program</a>. In a language model it comes from the text itself: the next word of a sentence is covered up and the model is asked to guess it. Since the text already carries the answer, nobody has to label anything, and that is why it could be trained on an enormous part of what is written on the internet.

What is left at the end is called a <strong>model</strong>: a recipe the machine has written by itself after trying millions of dishes, which works but which nobody, not even those who trained it, can read line by line. If it was trained on text and is very big, it is an <strong>LLM</strong>, short for large language model. ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are LLMs with a chat window in front.

The only thing an LLM can do is <strong>guess what comes next</strong> in a text. It is what your phone's keyboard does when you type “good” and it suggests “morning”, but having read an entire library and taking the whole conversation into account. It guesses a piece, adds it and starts again; piece by piece, a paragraph comes out.

I say piece because the model does not read words but <a href="https://platform.openai.com/tokenizer"><strong>tokens</strong></a>: fragments that are sometimes a whole word, sometimes half of one and sometimes a punctuation mark. It looks like an engineering detail, but it explains three things any user notices: that companies <strong>charge by tokens</strong>; that each model can only handle a limited number at a time, and that is why it forgets the beginning of long conversations; and that it has trouble counting the letters in a word. It doesn't see letters, it sees pieces.

With this we can go back to the question of <a href="https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palacio_de_Sch%C3%B6nbrunn">Schönbrunn</a>. Inside the model there is nobody, but everything we people have written is there: books, articles, forums, arguments. And before opening it to the public, OpenAI turned to people who <a href="https://openai.com/index/chatgpt/">wrote example conversations and scored which answers were better</a>, to teach it manners. Whoever answers is not a man hidden away. It is an average of a great many people, and that explains the good and, as we will see, the bad.

That average has been remade in recent years at a speed that is hard to keep up with. You don't need to memorise the names; it is enough to notice the pace:

## Why it gets things wrong with such confidence

Now the key sentence makes sense: <strong>an LLM produces the most probable text, not the truest</strong>. Nobody has taught it what truth is; it has been taught how a text usually continues. Almost always the two coincide. Almost.

When they don't coincide, the model doesn't fall silent or say “I don't know”: it writes something that sounds perfect and is false. It is called a <strong>hallucination</strong>: a book that doesn't exist, a quotation nobody said. And it says it with the same confidence as what is true, because for it there is no difference. It happens most where there was least text to learn from: exact figures and dates, proper names, the very recent (it knows nothing after its training, unless the application searches the internet for it) and the very local. The best way to measure it is to ask it, without letting it search, about something you know in depth: the Falla final of a particular year, the history of your neighbourhood, the details of your trade. The mistakes you find there show how far to trust it on what you don't know.

<strong>Biases</strong> are the other problem, and they are not a flaw of the machine but a mirror. If in the texts it learned from nurses are almost always “she” and engineers “he”, the model will tend to repeat it. It has no prejudices of its own: it has ours, and sometimes magnified.

None of this makes it useless. It is good at working with words, which is what it is made of: summarising, explaining something difficult another way, making a first draft, translating. And, since it understands images, reading you an invoice or a sign in another language. It is bad at what demands being sure: deciding for you, a medical or legal diagnosis, replacing someone who knows. I use it to prepare the questions, not as the final answer, and what matters to me I check in another source.

## What you write goes into the cabinet too

What you write in a <em>chatbot</em> doesn't stay on your phone: it <strong>travels to a company's computers</strong>, almost always in another country, and in the best-known services, unless you switch it off, it may end up among the texts the next model is trained on. That is, inside the cabinet.

A simple rule: <strong>if you wouldn't say it out loud on the bus, don't write it</strong>. The Spanish Data Protection Agency published in January 2026 the decalogue <a href="https://www.aepd.es/prensa-y-comunicacion/notas-de-prensa/aepd-publica-decalogo-recomendaciones-proteger-privacidad-al-usar-ia">“Cuidado con lo que le confIAs”</a>, which goes along the same lines: no ID number, no address, no medical or bank details, no photos of other people, even less of minors, no confidential information from work. And it asks you to check the answers and not to replace a professional with a chatbot, not even in emotional matters.

The three most used services have settings for this. In ChatGPT, the option <a href="https://help.openai.com/es-es/articles/7730893-preguntas-frecuentes-sobre-controles-de-datos">“Improve the model for everyone”</a>, inside Data controls, can be switched off, and temporary chats are deleted after 30 days. In Claude, Anthropic <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/news/updates-to-our-consumer-terms">asks each user</a> since late 2025 whether they allow their conversations to be used for training: if they accept, they are kept for up to five years; if not, 30 days. In Gemini, the setting is called “Keep Activity” and <a href="https://blog.google/intl/es-419/actualizaciones-de-producto/informacion/gemini-incorpora-chats-temporales-y-nuevas-funciones-de-personalizacion/">temporary chats</a> are deleted after 72 hours. It is worth spending five minutes looking at them.

In addition, the European Union has had since 2024 an <a href="https://www.europarl.europa.eu/topics/es/article/20230601STO93804/ley-de-ia-de-la-ue-primera-normativa-sobre-inteligencia-artificial">artificial intelligence regulation</a> that applies in phases. Since February 2025 it bans some uses, such as the social scoring of citizens, and <a href="https://www.economistjurist.es/zbloque-1/reglamento-europeo-de-ia-tras-el-omnibus-que-obligaciones-y-sanciones-ya-son-exigibles-en-agosto-de-2026/">since 2 August 2026</a> it requires a system to tell you that you are talking to a machine and to label generated images and videos. The obligations for high-risk systems, those that decide on a loan, a job or a public benefit, were postponed in July 2026 until December 2027.

## Lady Lovelace's objection

In 1843, <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0TCpHlsBjSs">Ada Lovelace</a> warned that Babbage's machine “has no pretensions whatever to originate anything”. A century later, Turing replied to her with another idea: not to program an adult mind, but a “child machine” that could be educated. Both were right, and together they describe fairly well what is behind a chat window today.

The child machine exists. It has been educated on almost everything we have written, and that is why it speaks like us, gets things right like us and gets things wrong like us. But Lovelace's objection still stands: what it says it does not originate. It was originated by the people who wrote before, those who corrected it afterwards and the one who now types the question. In Schönbrunn, the audience looked at the figure when they should have looked at whoever sat on the other side of the board, because that person decided which move was worth making. With artificial intelligence, that person is you.
